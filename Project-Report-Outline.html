Introduction

Ecosystems around the world face many threats — things like habitat loss, climate change, and pollution (Gentili et al. 2021). One problem that people often overlook is the spread of invasive species (USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service 2024). In New York State, this isn’t just a future worry. It’s something that scientists, state agencies, and local communities deal with every day (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b).

This report uses field data from the Lower Hudson Partnership for Regional Invasive Species Management (LHPRISM) to look at where invasive species are found in the Lower Hudson Valley and how they are being managed (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b). To understand why this data matters, it helps to know the bigger picture: what invasive species are, how New York State organizes its response, and what official plans guide decisions about which species to focus on.

Background on Invasive Species

At its most basic, an invasive species is any organism — plant, animal, insect, fungus, or microbe — that establishes itself outside its native range and causes meaningful harm to the environment, economy, or human health (Mack et al. 2000; USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service 2024). The word “invasive” is doing a lot of work in that definition. Not every non-native species causes problems; in fact, most introduced species fail to survive at all in new environments (Mack et al. 2000). They arrive without the adaptations needed to handle unfamiliar climates, predators, and food sources, and they quietly die out. The ones that do establish themselves and thrive are the exception — but when they do, the consequences can be severe and long-lasting (Gentili et al. 2021).

What makes certain species so successful in new environments often comes down to a lack of natural checks (Mack et al. 2000). Back in their home range, they are kept in balance by predators, competitors, parasites, and other ecological forces that have co-evolved alongside them over thousands of years. When they land somewhere new, those controls disappear (Mack et al. 2000). Native species in the invaded area have no evolutionary history with this newcomer and may not recognize it as a threat, compete with it effectively, or survive in its presence (USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service 2024). The invasive species, freed from its usual constraints, can reproduce rapidly, consume resources aggressively, and spread far beyond where it first appeared (Gentili et al. 2021).

The pathways through which invasive species move around the world are closely tied to human activity (Mack et al. 2000). Global trade is one of the biggest drivers — cargo ships, shipping containers, and pallets of imported goods routinely carry hitchhiking organisms across continents and oceans (USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service 2024). The pet trade has introduced countless exotic animals into new regions, and when those animals escape or are deliberately released, some find conditions favorable enough to establish wild populations (USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service 2024). Ornamental horticulture has a long history of introducing plants that eventually escape garden boundaries and spread into natural areas (Mack et al. 2000). Even recreational activities like hiking, boating, and fishing can accidentally transport seeds, larvae, or spores from one watershed to another (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b).

The ecological damage caused by established invasive species takes many forms (Gentili et al. 2021). Some are aggressive predators that decimate native prey populations before those populations have any chance to adapt (Mack et al. 2000). Others are competitors that crowd out native plants or animals from food, space, and other resources they need to survive (USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service 2024). Over time, a successful invasion can fundamentally restructure an ecosystem — replacing native plant communities with monocultures, disrupting food webs, altering water cycles, and in the most severe cases, driving native species to local or even global extinction (Gentili et al. 2021; Mack et al. 2000). Invasive species can also carry parasites and pathogens that spill over into native wildlife, compounding the damage furtherndefined [(us?)

Beyond the ecological toll, invasive species impose enormous economic costs (Diagne et al. 2021). Agricultural industries lose billions of dollars annually to crop damage from invasive pests and weeds (Diagne et al. 2021). Forestry operations contend with invasive insects and diseases that kill commercially valuable trees (Mack et al. 2000). Water infrastructure is disrupted by organisms like zebra mussels that clog intake pipes and alter aquatic chemistry (USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service 2024). And the cost of active management — detection, treatment, removal, and restoration — adds yet another layer to the financial burden (Diagne et al. 2021; Leung et al. 2002). In densely populated and ecologically diverse states like New York, these costs are felt across sectors and communities (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b).

New York is particularly exposed to invasive species pressure for several interconnected reasons (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b). It sits at the hub of one of the busiest transportation networks on the eastern seaboard, with major ports, airports, highways, and rail lines that move enormous volumes of goods and people (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b). Its landscape spans a remarkable range of ecosystems — coastal wetlands, deciduous forests, alpine zones, freshwater river systems — each of which represents a potential foothold for a different suite of invasive organisms (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b). And its long history of settlement, agriculture, and land use transformation has already altered many native habitats in ways that make them more vulnerable to invasion (Gentili et al. 2021). Put all of that together, and it becomes clear why New York has invested significantly in building a statewide infrastructure for invasive species management (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b).

Background on NYS DEC Invasive Species Programs and the PRISM System

The New York State Department of Environmental Conservation (DEC) works with many groups — such as resource managers, non-profit organizations, industry workers, recreation groups, and other state agencies — to deal with invasive species (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b). To help with this work, New York created eight Partnerships for Regional Invasive Species Management (PRISMs) (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b). These PRISMs cover the entire state and help reduce the damage invasive species cause to New York’s environment (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2016). PRISMs were formed and funded under New York State law (Environmental Conservation Law 9-1705) (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b).

PRISMs are groups of partners who work together to manage invasive species in different regions of New York (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2016). They include people from federal and state agencies, environmental groups, scientists, industry, outdoor recreation, and local community members (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b). The DEC supports each PRISM through the Environmental Protection Fund, giving money to the organizations that host and coordinate them (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b, 2016).

Since 2013, the DEC has contracted organizations to run all eight PRISMs across the state, which plan and coordinate regional invasive species management while bringing partners together statewide (NYSDEC2016?). They survey and map invasions, detecting new threats early and responding quickly before species spread (NYSDEC2016?). Control and removal projects are carried out alongside habitat restoration and monitoring after treatment (NYSDEC2024?). Communities are engaged through education, outreach, volunteer training, and citizen science support through the Invasive Species Research Institute, with sightings logged to iMapInvasives (NYSDEC2024?).

PRISMs provide comprehensive regional planning for invasive species management, ensuring coordinated strategies are in place across all eight regions of New York State (NYSDEC2016?). They lead early detection and rapid response efforts, minimizing the establishment and spread of new invasions (NYSDEC2016?). Eradication and control projects are executed on the ground, followed by long-term habitat restoration and monitoring to track recovery (NYSDEC2024?). With DEC-supported educators, PRISMs deliver targeted education and outreach to communities, raising awareness and building local capacity (NYSDEC2024?)

The DEC is the main state agency responsible for environmental protection in New York, and invasive species management is part of its work (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b). Because invasive species spread across boundaries, New York created PRISMs so each region can coordinate its efforts (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b).

The Lower Hudson PRISM (LHPRISM) covers the Lower Hudson Valley (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b). It uses trained staff and community volunteers who go into the field, record invasive species, help with treatment projects, and upload data to a shared statewide database (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b). This data is used for many environmental projects in the region (Finley et al. 2023).

Information and Background on the NYS Invasive Species Lists

Not every non-native species that shows up in New York needs immediate action (Leung et al. 2002). Deciding which species deserve attention — and how urgent that attention should be — requires a clear system for evaluating risk (Leung et al. 2002). New York State uses two main systems to do this: a regulatory list managed by the Department of Environmental Conservation (DEC) and a tiered priority system created through collaboration between the PRISM network, state agencies, and the New York Natural Heritage Program (NYNHP) (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b; New York Natural Heritage Program 2024).

The regulatory list, created under the Environmental Conservation Law, sorts species into two groups based on how harmful they are and what restrictions are needed (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b).

Prohibited species are considered so damaging, and so unnecessary for any legal use, that they cannot be possessed, sold, bought, transported, or introduced in New York at all (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b). There are no permits for these species — they are simply banned (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b).

Regulated species are more complicated (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b). They may have some allowed uses, but there are still rules in place to prevent them from spreading (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b).

Before a species is added to either category, experts review scientific information about its ecological impacts, how quickly it spreads, how feasible it is to control, and any economic or social considerations (Leung et al. 2002; New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b).

Alongside this legal framework, NYNHP created a tiering system to help PRISMs and other managers decide where to focus their limited time and resources (New York Natural Heritage Program 2024). Because New York has hundreds of invasive species, the tiering system uses a structured method that combines iMapInvasives data, invasiveness assessments, and expert input (Finley et al. 2023). A species only receives a tier if it is considered to have high negative impacts (New York Natural Heritage Program 2024). Species that are present but not very harmful do not get tier assignments (New York Natural Heritage Program 2024). The tier definitions were finalized in 2017 through a statewide collaboration, giving all PRISMs a shared set of priorities (New York Natural Heritage Program 2024; Finley et al. 2023).

These tiers can change over time (New York Natural Heritage Program 2024). They depend on accurate data, so new reports or successful management efforts can shift a species’ tier (Finley et al. 2023). This is why reporting new sightings to iMapInvasives is strongly encouraged — better data leads to better decisions (New York Natural Heritage Program 2024). For the species included in this report, their presence in the LHPRISM dataset means they have already been evaluated, found to be high-impact, and identified as priorities for management in the Lower Hudson region (Finley et al. 2023; New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b).

Understanding this background — the regulatory status, the tier level, and the management history of each species — is important for interpreting the data and understanding what it means for ongoing conservation work in this part of New York State (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b; New York Natural Heritage Program 2024).

Region of study - Tanaka

The study area for this project encompasses the Lower Hudson region, as delineated by the Lower Hudson Partnership for Regional Invasive Species Management (LH PRISM). Established as one of eight regional invasive species management groups across New York State, LH PRISM coordinates collaborative efforts among government agencies, conservation organizations, and landowners to monitor and manage invasive species at a regional scale (Lower Hudson Partnership for Regional Invasive Species Management 2024). The region includes Westchester, Dutchess, Putnam, Rockland, Orange, and Ulster counties, extending southward into the five boroughs of New York City (Lower Hudson Partnership for Regional Invasive Species Management 2024; New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b).

Geographically, the region is anchored by the Hudson River estuary, a highly dynamic tidal system in which freshwater inputs from inland watersheds interact with saltwater intrusion from the Atlantic Ocean. This estuarine gradient sustains a broad array of habitat types, including tidal wetlands, riparian corridors, freshwater marshes, and open aquatic environments, collectively rendering the Lower Hudson one of the most ecologically productive systems in the northeastern United States (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024a). The region also functions as a critical landscape transition zone, bridging the heavily urbanized New York City metropolitan area with the more rural and forested landscapes of the mid-Hudson Valley. This gradient of land use intensities is particularly significant in the context of biological invasions, as anthropogenic disturbance is well-documented as a primary driver of invasive species establishment and spread (Cornell University Department of Natural Resources and the Environment 2024; New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b).

Climate

The Lower Hudson region is characterized by a humid temperate climate with four well-defined seasons. Summers are warm and humid, winters are cold with periodic snowfall, and precipitation is distributed relatively uniformly throughout the year, averaging approximately 40 to 50 inches annually (National Centers for Environmental Information 2024). These climatic conditions support a wide diversity of native vegetation communities while simultaneously creating favorable thermal and moisture regimes for the establishment of non-native invasive species (Kunkel et al. 2013; National Centers for Environmental Information 2024). Notably, climate projections for the northeastern United States indicate continued warming trends and increasing precipitation variability, conditions that are expected to further facilitate the range expansion of invasive species into previously unsuitable habitats (Kunkel et al. 2013).

Ecology

The Lower Hudson region falls within the Eastern Temperate Forest biome and supports a mosaic of ecological communities, including mixed deciduous forests, freshwater wetlands, floodplain systems, and estuarine habitats (New York Natural Heritage Program 2021). Dominant forest canopy species include oak (Quercus spp.), maple (Acer spp.), and hickory (Carya spp.), which provide structural complexity and support high levels of faunal biodiversity across the landscape (New York Natural Heritage Program 2021; Cornell University Department of Natural Resources and the Environment 2024). The region’s network of river systems and wetlands further contributes to its ecological significance, serving as corridors for both native wildlife and, unfortunately, the dispersal of invasive plant species (New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024a).

The Lower Hudson region presents an ideal study area for analyzing invasive species distribution patterns given its unique combination of ecological diversity, landscape heterogeneity, and anthropogenic pressure. The juxtaposition of urban and natural land cover types creates conditions highly conducive to invasive species introduction, as disturbance-adapted non-native plants readily exploit habitat edges, degraded areas, and human-modified landscapes (Lower Hudson Partnership for Regional Invasive Species Management 2024; New York State Department of Environmental Conservation 2024b). The dataset employed in this study, compiled through field surveys conducted by the Invasives Strike Force under the coordination of LH PRISM, provides spatially explicit records of invasive plant populations across a broad range of property types within this region. Species identifications in the dataset are standardized using codes from the USDA PLANTS Database, ensuring taxonomic consistency across observation records (USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service 2024). Together, the ecological characteristics of the Lower Hudson region and the availability of a robust regional dataset make this an appropriate and well-suited geographic context for the analyses undertaken in this project.

Overview of the LHPRISM data set

Dataset overview

The dataset is an environmental monitoring record compiled by the Lower Hudson Partnership for Regional Invasive Species Management (LHPRISM). This organization operates within the broader NY-NJ region. Records span from 1992 to 2022, meaning we do not have a static snapshot of the region, but instead an archive of ongoing invasive management. This dataset is concerned with invasive plant species and the coordinated effort to control and eliminate such species from targeted sites across the northeast. Both the collection period and geographical range are broad, with records drawn from over a dozen counties. Sites range from parks, wildlife sanctuaries, golf courses, and nature preserves. Such a swath reflects the extent of ecological damage caused by invasive species.

The dataset comprises just under 1,050 distinct sites that are each assigned a unique code and linked to contact information. The ecological content is split into populations and visits. The Populations tab documents 2,127 individual invasive species infestations that are assigned a specific property code and plant species code, georeferenced with latitude and longitude coordinates, and assigned a current treatment status. These statuses range from “Untreated” to “Eradicated”, with intermediate stages including “Partially Treated,” “Completely Treated,” “No Plants 1 Year,” and “No Plants 2 Years.” This graduated classification system is central to the dataset’s design, as it is precisely this progression, or lack of progression, that the dataset was built to track.

The species catalog is diverse, containing 86 unique species across the region. Some of the more predominant invasives include multiflora rose, Japanese knotweed, and mile-a-minute vine. The Visit sheets contain nearly 3,830 records representing the field backbone of the dataset. Each visit log captures the site surveyed, the date of the survey, the person performing the survey, and the conditions of the site. Variables record plant count, percent cover, phenological stage, the size of the area treated, and herbicide application specifics such as product name, concentration, and method used. This operational data is where the utility for evaluation arises, since it also tracks each site over time. This dataset is a collaboration between at least 18 distinct organizations. That collaboration is a key feature of the dataset and introduces meaningful variation in the data, as it may reflect what fields were prioritized and the consistency of terminology used. For example, the appearance of both “2-Completely Treated” and “Completely Treated 2” as distinct entries shows that standardization was not always consistent.

Prior to data analysis, our team completed a data cleaning procedure on OpenRefine to ensure consistency across entry variations. Missingness in variables pertaining to plant count and cover may carry more weight than treatment method variables, since every record likely did not undergo every possible treatment, but every record should have had a baseline count value to assess treatment efficiency. Variables with higher percentages of missing data may still be useful, but may also lead to gaps in final analysis.

Uncertainty caused by gaps in this dataset primarily originates from a lack of direct communication with LHPRISM staff. Our ability to confirm procedural methods has been limited, including procedure design, implementation, and standardization across individuals visiting these sites. Though not the primary focus of our analysis, several assumptions were made for categorical variables:

  • Variables addressing percent buckets likely follow traditional ecological methods in which observers categorize visual estimates into 0–5, 6–25, 26–50, 51–75, and 76–100 percent classes.
  • Distribution, native, and non_nat variables likely followed the same concept; observers probably did rapid visual assessments for category classification rather than taking numerical measures.
  • Phenology and age_size were likely classified by assessing visible physical characteristics and size of vegetation.

It is unknown how sites were selected and defined. The data does not show whether sites were chosen randomly or selected because of high invasive presence. It is also possible that sites were chosen opportunistically for accessibility or to support quick evaluation of treatment efficiency. Not knowing how locations were chosen introduces probable bias. For example, a dataset selected because of denser invasive populations could skew results toward harder-to-treat sites, which would make treatment efficacy appear lower than it would across a random selection of sites. Multiple observers visited unique sites over time, which may affect consistency in visual assessments of percent_cover or similar non-standardized variables. Additionally, there is no information on whether observers were responding to the same sub-area within one site, as search_sqft and gross_sqft differ across recordings under one site code. We are unsure whether these inconsistencies reflect opportunity to visit or intentional collaboration. Variation in data entry across individual observers has the potential to affect analysis, since gaps may come from differences in technique rather than true ecological response.

Categories in visit_status introduce a heavier uncertainty because we are unsure whether observers were prompted to record the site’s current treatment condition or only document work completed during that specific visit. The sequence of entries for a site on the Appalachian Trail regarding sticky sage suggests this ambiguity: one record lists the plot as completely treated, but a later visit by a different observer lists it as untreated. This pattern suggests that observers may have recorded their own actions rather than a full treatment history. If so, reversions in status could distort analyses about the time required to move a species toward eradication. A site listed as untreated because no new work was performed could appear to regress in the treatment timeline, even if the actual ecological condition had not changed.

The overall purpose of the dataset is to evaluate the effectiveness of treatment interventions over time and to estimate how long it takes to move a species toward eradication. By tracking multiple sites over multiple years, the dataset highlights important questions: Which species respond best to intervention? Which species persist despite repeated treatment? Under what conditions are populations most effectively reduced or eradicated? Beyond individual population outcomes, the dataset is intended to surface broader patterns, trends in species spread, shifts in treatment methods, and regional variation in management success that can inform future resource allocation and strategic planning for invasive species control across the lower Hudson Valley.

The Leaflet JavaScript library (INSERT LEAFLET REFS) was used to create two maps for this dataset: one depicting the exact locations of each population that was treated and another displaying their treatment statuses. Map two describes the extent to which the populations at each location were treated using a color coordination scheme. These maps provide a tidy visual aid for users by offering a straightforward presentation with a useful interface that allows users to learn more about any data point they choose.

The second map uses a color gradient to classify points based on how far along the invasive species at these locations are in their treatment. The labels used are “Untreated,” “Partially Treated,” “Completely Treated,” “No plants for 1 year,” “No plants for 2 years,” and “Eradicated.” Untreated sites are grey, partially treated sites are orange-red, completely treated sites are yellow, sites with no plants for a year are yellow-green, sites with no plants for two years are green, and sites that have been eradicated of invasive plants are represented with lime green. This gradient provides a display that is easy for readers to understand and follow along with.

  • alters nutrient cycle by nitrogen fixation

  • treatment complex, requires multi-modal approach over multiple years

  • 1 plant produces 1,000s of seeds
